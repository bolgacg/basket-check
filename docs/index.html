What you see. Every fund's holdings file for one day, summed and set against the fund's value as the issuer published it, three ways, one strip each. How to read it. One mark is one fund, grouped by kind along the bottom. Its height is the gap in basis points, hundredths of a percent: above the line means the file adds up to more than the fund. Before going further

Five things to know

This page reads one day of fund files, so here is what is in them and what is being checked. Nothing after this assumes you have seen an ETF file before.

  1. An ETF is a fund whose units trade on the stock exchange. Once a day, after the close, the issuer works out what one unit is worth: the fund's assets, minus what it owes, divided by the units on issue. That figure is the NAV, net asset value.
  2. Market makers quote the ETF all day from a basket: the holdings and cash that one creation unit, a large block of new units, stands for. The file that lists it is the PCF, portfolio composition file. Valued at live prices it gives the indicative NAV, the running estimate of what a unit is worth.
  3. Issuers also publish each fund's full holdings: one line per holding, with a quantity and a market value in Australian dollars. BetaShares serves only the latest file for each fund, and the full daily history of its NAV.
  4. If the file is complete and dated like the NAV, its market values add up to the NAV times the units on issue. Any gap is something the file does not say. It is counted in basis points: one basis point is a hundredth of a percent, about one dollar in every ten thousand.
  5. Three things break that identity: a date that differs between the file and the figure it is checked against, a line printed on a different basis from the NAV, such as a bond without the interest it has earned, and a holding that moves the NAV without carrying a value in the file, such as a futures contract.

Terms, in the order they appear
basis point (bp) A hundredth of a percent.
units on issue How many units of the fund exist. It changes when market makers create or redeem units in blocks. Each fund page gives the number with an as at date.
net assets The fund's total value, NAV times units on issue, as printed on its page with its own as at date.
coupon The interest a bond pays, as a percentage of its face value a year, here in two payments six months apart.
face value The amount a bond repays at maturity, and the amount its coupon is calculated on.
clean price and accrued interest Bond prices are quoted clean, without the interest earned since the last coupon. A fund owns that interest, so its NAV includes it.
floating rate note A bond whose coupon resets every quarter. The files mark these with Float in the code.
index futures Contracts on a share index. Gains and losses settle in cash each day, so the file lists the contract with a quantity and no value.
geared fund A fund that borrows to hold more than its own value in shares. The loan shows as negative cash.
fund of funds A fund whose holdings are other ETFs.
hybrid A security between a bond and a share, here bank and insurer issues listed on the exchange.
options overlay A share fund that also sells options on its holdings for income.

Method, choices and sources

This page checking itself

The headline could have been typed by hand. So the page ships each fund's sums, recomputes every gap and the break counts in your browser now, and prints the result beside what the build script wrote.

Running.

How the check works

  1. Collect, for every fund listed on the issuer's fund pages, its latest holdings file, its NAV history and the units on issue and net assets its page shows.
  2. Add up the file's Market Value column. Lines with no value, such as futures, add nothing.
  3. As printed: set the sum against the net assets on the fund's page. Dated: set it against the NAV on the file's own date, from the NAV history, times the units on issue.
  4. Mine: as dated, after adding to every fixed rate bond the interest it has earned since its last coupon, from the file's own coupon, maturity and face value columns, assuming two coupons a year counted back from maturity.
  5. The published figures are read last, only to grade the sum.

Limits

The issuer's own values are the only prices used. If a value in the file were wrong in the same way as the NAV, the check would pass.

The coupon rule assumes every fixed rate bond pays twice a year on its maturity day and month. After it, the bond funds still differ from their NAV by 10 to 40 bp, by amounts that change from day to day; the file does not say why.

Fund families are assigned from what each file holds and from the fund's name. The likely causes in the checklist are labelled as likely where the files cannot prove them.
